Chocolate Dough: The Crust That Becomes a Craving

I am not a chocolate person.

I want to be upfront about that because it matters for what I'm about to tell you. I don't reach for chocolate desserts. I don't keep chocolate in the house. If you put a brownie and a slice of pizza in front of me, I am taking the pizza without a second thought every single time.

So when I tell you that I made a chocolate pizza dough for my birthday, ate the dessert pizza it produced for three nights in a row, and found myself genuinely craving it by the time it was gone, I need you to understand what that means coming from me. This dough did something to me. And I think it's going to do something to you too.

The idea started simply enough. I wanted to do something creative for my birthday pizza session. I'd been thinking about infused doughs, doughs where the crust itself carries a flavor rather than just acting as a neutral vehicle for toppings, and the concept of a chocolate dough had been sitting in the back of my mind. Chocolate and spice have a long history together: mole sauce, cayenne in truffles, Mexican hot chocolate. The marriage of bitter and heat is one of the great flavor combinations in cooking and I wanted to put it in a crust.

What I didn't expect was what happened when I opened that dough container Wednesday night. It smelled like chocolate. Not like a brownie, not like a candy bar, but like chocolate bread. That warm, deep, slightly bitter cocoa smell layered underneath the familiar yeast note of a cold-fermenting pizza dough. The yeast smell was almost secondary. It just smelled like something worth eating.

That smell was the first sign this was going to be different.

What This Dough Actually Is

Before we get into the recipe, it's worth being clear about what a chocolate pizza dough is and what it isn't, because I think the concept trips people up before they even start.

This is not a sweet dough. It's not a brownie base or a dessert crust in the way that phrase might make you picture something sugary and cake-like. It's a savory bread dough with cocoa worked into it, using the same cold ferment NY style process that produces The Rowdy Pizza's standard crust, modified to carry chocolate flavor without losing any of its structural integrity or baking performance.

The result is a crust that tastes like chocolate bread: slightly bitter, deeply savory, with a complexity that plain flour dough simply doesn't have. It holds up to toppings exactly the way the standard NY style does. It blisters on the steel, it gets color and char at the edges, it has chew and structure. It just also happens to taste like chocolate.

That combination of familiar pizza dough behavior and unexpected chocolate flavor is what makes it so compelling. Your brain is expecting pizza and getting something more interesting.

The Espresso Powder Questions

There's a teaspoon of espresso powder in this dough and I want to address it directly because it raises a reasonable question: does this taste like coffee?

It does not. Not even slightly.

Espresso powder in baking isn't there to add coffee flavor. It's there because coffee and chocolate are molecularly complementary. The compounds in coffee amplify and deepen chocolate flavor in a way that reads as "more chocolate" rather than "coffee." Bakers have been doing this for decades in cakes and brownies for exactly this reason. In this dough the espresso powder is invisible as a flavor. What it leaves behind is a chocolate that tastes more rounded, more complex, and more distinctly itself. You would never identify it as an ingredient. You would notice if it wasn't there.

The Cocoa Powder Decision

This recipe uses Hershey's natural unsweetened cocoa powder, which is what I had on hand and what most people will find at their grocery store without hunting. Natural cocoa is slightly more acidic and sharper than Dutch-process cocoa, which has been alkalized to produce a smoother, rounder chocolate flavor. If you can find Dutch-process cocoa it will give you a slightly more mellow and sophisticated chocolate note in the final crust. If you're working with Hershey's natural cocoa like I was, it works beautifully. Just know the flavor will be a little more assertive and slightly bitter at the edges, which for a pizza crust is actually not a problem at all.

What matters most is using unsweetened cocoa. This is not a sweetened dough and sweetened cocoa powder will throw the entire balance off.

How the Dough Handles

If you've made The Rowdy Pizza NY style dough before, this dough will feel familiar in your hands. Same mixing process, same cold ferment, same 2-hour room temperature rest before shaping. The cocoa powder replaces a portion of the bread flour rather than adding to it, so the hydration ratio stays balanced and the dough doesn't behave dramatically differently from what you're used to.

One thing worth noting: this dough will look very dark. Almost alarmingly dark when you first mix it, like something went wrong. Nothing went wrong. That's cocoa doing exactly what it's supposed to do. By the time it comes out of the oven the crust will be a deep, rich brown that photographs beautifully against bright toppings.

The one variable I encountered in my session was slight snap-back when shaping, which I attribute entirely to not giving the dough enough time to come to room temperature before working with it. I'd been out of town and pulled it from the refrigerator later than ideal. The dough itself wasn't the issue; the handling was. Give it the full 2 hours at room temperature and it will stretch and shape the same way your standard NY style does.

What to Make With It

This dough opened two completely different creative directions on the same birthday session, and that versatility is the most important thing to understand about it.

The savory direction takes the chocolate into unexpected territory, specifically the mole tradition of pairing chocolate with chilies and warm spice. The birthday session produced a mole chicken pizza on this crust that was genuinely unlike anything I've made before. A Note on Same-Day Baking

The cold ferment process, 48–72 hours in the refrigerator, is what gives this dough its flavor depth and structural strength, and it's what I always recommend. The long slow ferment develops complexity that a same-day dough simply cannot replicate.

That said, if you need this dough today, here's how to adapt it: increase the instant yeast from 4g to 8g, use slightly warm water around 95°F rather than room temperature, mix and knead as directed, then let the dough rest at room temperature for 2 hours before shaping. The flavor will be simpler and the texture slightly less developed, but the chocolate character will still come through and the dough will still perform. Think of the same-day version as the weeknight shortcut and the cold ferment version as the one worth planning for.

At a Glance

  • Makes: two 11-12 inch pizzas
  • Active time: 25 minutes

The Recipe

Ingredient Weight Volume
Bread flour 420g 3½ cups
Cocoa powder, unsweetened 30g ¼ cup
Water, room temperature 270g 1⅛ + 1 tbs
Instant Yeast 4g 1¼ tsp
Salt 10g 1¾ tsp
Sugar 14g 1 tbsp
Espresso powder 3g 1 tsp
Olive oil 14g 1 tbsp
Olive oil for containers As needed As needed
  1. Mix the dry ingredients. Whisk together the bread flour, cocoa powder, instant yeast, salt, sugar, and espresso powder in a large bowl until fully combined. The cocoa needs to be evenly distributed before any liquid is added. Take an extra moment here.
  2. Add water and oil. Pour in the room temperature water and olive oil. Mix until no dry flour remains and a shaggy dough forms.
  3. Knead. Turn out onto a lightly floured surface and knead for 8–10 minutes until the dough is smooth, elastic, and slightly tacky but not sticky. The dough will be very dark. This is correct.
  4. Divide and container. Divide the dough into two equal portions. Shape each into a tight ball and place in lightly oiled containers with lids.
  5. Cold ferment. Refrigerate for 48–72 hours. 72 hours produces the best flavor development and is worth the wait.
  6. Room temperature rest. Remove from the refrigerator at least 2 hours before baking. The dough needs to fully relax and come to room temperature before shaping. Do not rush this step.
  7. Shape and bake. Shape and bake according to your chosen pizza recipe. For the dessert application, bake the crust alone at 500°F on the pizza steel for 6–8 minutes until set and slightly charred at the edges, then cool completely before adding toppings.
