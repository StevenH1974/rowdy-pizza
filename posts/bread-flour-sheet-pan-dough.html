Bread Flour Sheet Pan Dough: The Recipe

This is the dough that changed my Friday nights.

It came out of weeks of frustrating experiments, shaggy messes, and dough that fought me at every turn. What I eventually understood about bread making, gluten, fermentation, hydration, environment, all of it lives in this recipe.

This bread flour sheet pan dough is soft, airy, and forgiving enough for a home kitchen while being structured enough to hold whatever toppings you put on it. It needs time more than anything else. Plan to make it 6–8 hours before you want to eat. That's the secret. That's always been the secret.

At a Glance

  • Makes: one standard half sheet pan (18×13 inches)
  • Active time: 15 minutes
  • Rest time: 6-8 hours
  • Oven temp: 450°F
  • Bake time: 18-22 minutes

Ingredients

Ingredient Weight Volume
Bread Flour 450g 3¾ cups
Warm Water (105°F) 290g 1¼ cups + 2 tbsp
Instant Yeast 7g 1 packet (2¼ tsp)
Salt 9g 1½ tsp
Sugar 8g 2 tsp
Olive Oil (in dough) 28g 2 tbsp
Olive Oil (for bowl) As needed As needed
Olive Oil (for pan and top) As needed Generous

Ingredient Notes

Bread Flour

Bread flour has a higher protein content than all-purpose flour, which means it builds a stronger gluten network, giving your crust that satisfying chew and structure. It's widely available, affordable, and forgiving to work with once you understand how it behaves.

Instant Yeast

Also called fast-acting or rapid-rise yeast. Instant yeast doesn't need to be activated in water before using. It goes straight into your dry ingredients and gets to work once it meets moisture. One standard packet is 7g (2¼ tsp).

Salt

Salt strengthens the gluten network and controls fermentation rate. Don't add it directly on top of your yeast before mixing. Direct contact can inhibit yeast activity.

Sugar

A small amount of sugar feeds the yeast and encourages fermentation activity. It also contributes to browning on the crust edges during baking.

Olive Oil

Goes into the dough itself for tenderness, into the bowl during the ferment, and generously into the pan before baking. That last application is what gives sheet pan pizza its signature crispy bottom crust.

Equipment

Kitchen Scale

Weigh your ingredients. Volume measuring introduces inconsistency that makes it nearly impossible to diagnose problems or replicate success. A basic digital kitchen scale runs $10–$15 and will change the way you bake. Look for one with a 5kg capacity and 1g resolution.

Large Mixing Bowl with a Lid

The bowl needs to be large enough to accommodate the dough roughly doubling during fermentation. A fitted lid keeps the surface from drying out. A damp towel works as a substitute.

Half Sheet Pan

A standard 18×13-inch rimmed baking sheet. Be generous with olive oil on newer or lighter-colored aluminum pans. Lighter pans don't retain heat as aggressively and need that oil layer working harder to achieve a crispy bottom. The pan performs better and better as it darkens and seasons over time.

Process Tips: What to Watch For

Before You Mix

Weigh everything before you start. Have your scale, bowl, and a clean surface ready. Check your yeast expiration date. Old yeast is the most common reason dough fails to rise.

During the Knead

The dough will look rough, shaggy, and almost broken for the first few minutes. Do not add more flour or water. Keep working it. Around the 5-minute mark it will begin to smooth out. At 10 minutes you're looking for a ball that is smooth, slightly tacky but not sticky, and springs back slowly when you press a finger into it.

That slow spring back is your signal to stop.

During the Ferment

Cover the bowl and leave it alone. When you uncover it after 6–8 hours you should see a dough that has roughly doubled in size, feels soft and pillowy to the touch, and has visible air bubbles just beneath the surface.

On the Pan

Be gentle. Turn the dough out carefully onto your well-oiled pan and use your fingertips to press it outward. If it resists and springs back, cover it and walk away for 15–20 minutes. Come back and try again. Forcing it tears the dough and works against everything the ferment built.

The Final Rest

Once the dough reaches the edges, drizzle generously with olive oil, cover loosely, and rest for 45 minutes to 1 hour before topping and baking. This final rest allows the olive oil to work its way underneath. That's what creates the crispy Sicilian-style bottom crust.

The Recipe

  1. Mix the dry ingredients. In a large bowl, whisk together the bread flour, instant yeast, sugar, and salt. Make sure the salt and yeast are evenly distributed throughout the flour before adding any liquid.
  2. Add the wet ingredients. Add the olive oil to your warm water and pour it into the dry ingredients. Mix with a wooden spoon or your hands until a shaggy, rough dough forms. It will look broken and flaky at this stage. That is completely normal. Do not add more flour or water.
  3. Knead. Turn the dough out onto a clean surface. If it sticks, add no more than 1 tbsp of flour to the surface. Knead for 8–10 minutes until the dough is smooth, slightly tacky but not sticky, and springs back slowly when you press a finger into it.
  4. Bulk ferment. Coat a large lidded bowl generously with olive oil. Place the dough ball inside, turn it once to coat, cover, and leave at room temperature for 6–8 hours. The dough should roughly double in size and show visible air bubbles when uncovered. If your kitchen runs cool, place the bowl in your oven with just the oven light on.
  5. Transfer to the pan. Pour a generous amount of olive oil into your half sheet pan. You want a visible pool across the entire bottom and up the sides. Gently turn the dough out onto the pan. Using your fingertips, press the dough outward toward the edges. If it resists and springs back, cover loosely and rest for 15–20 minutes. Return and press again. Repeat until the dough reaches the edges and corners.
  6. Final rest. Once the dough is spread, drizzle olive oil generously across the top surface. Cover loosely and rest for 45 minutes to 1 hour.
  7. Top and bake.Preheat your oven to 450°F during the final rest. Add your sauce, cheese, and toppings immediately after uncovering. Bake for 18–22 minutes until the edges are deep golden brown, the cheese is bubbling and beginning to spot, and the bottom releases cleanly from the pan when you lift a corner with a spatula.
  8. Rest before cutting. Remove from the oven and rest for 5 minutes before cutting. This allows the cheese to set and the crust structure to firm up slightly.

Storage and Make-Ahead

Leftover Baked Pizza

Store cooled slices in an airtight container in the refrigerator for up to 3 days. To reheat, skip the microwave. It makes the crust soft and chewy in all the wrong ways. Reheat slices in a skillet over medium heat with the lid on for 3–4 minutes. The bottom crisps back up and the cheese melts evenly.

Unbaked Dough

Portion the dough, coat lightly in olive oil, wrap tightly in plastic wrap, and refrigerate for up to 3 days or freeze for up to 3 months. Thaw frozen dough overnight in the refrigerator, then bring to room temperature for 1 hour before using.

Make-Ahead Strategy

Mix the dough the night before. Let it ferment at room temperature for 1 hour, then move it to the refrigerator for a slow cold ferment overnight. Pull it out 1 hour before you plan to use it the next day. The cold ferment develops even more complex flavor than a same-day room temperature ferment, and it's worth trying.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I use all-purpose flour instead of bread flour?

Yes, but the results will be different. All-purpose flour has a lower protein content, which means less gluten development, a softer and less chewy crust, and a dough that's more fragile to work with. It will still make a good pizza, just a different one. A dedicated all-purpose flour version is in development.

Can I make this dough without a scale?

Volume equivalents are included in the ingredients table. Just know that volume measuring introduces some inconsistency between batches. If your dough feels too wet or too dry, resist the urge to add more of anything until you've kneaded it for at least 5 minutes. Most issues resolve themselves through kneading.

My dough isn't doubling during the ferment. What's wrong?

  • Check the yeast expiration date. Old or inactive yeast is the most common culprit.
  • Check your water temperature. Aim for 105°F, warm to the touch but not hot. Water that's too hot kills yeast.
  • Check your kitchen temperature. Cool kitchens slow fermentation significantly. Try the oven-light trick.

Can I make this dough the night before?

Absolutely, and it's encouraged. Mix the dough, rest at room temperature for 1 hour, then refrigerate overnight. Pull it out 1 hour before use the next day. The cold ferment develops deeper flavor.

Why does my dough keep springing back when I try to stretch it?

It needs more rest. Cover it, walk away for 15–20 minutes, and try again. For a full explanation of why this happens: Why Your Pizza Dough Keeps Fighting You: And What's Actually Going On Inside It
